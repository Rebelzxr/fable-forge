# Recognising a generic AI-built site

A 30-item checklist of signs a page came from a template, plus additional tells. Apply it to website concepts, coding briefs and actual rendered pages. This is a pattern detector, not a forbidden-style list or a validated design score.

## How to use the check

- Read the actual brief and inspect the page. Count distinct visible patterns from the 30-item list below; do not count each repeated card as a separate item. Cite a concrete location for each finding. Mark anything not inspected as unknown.
- More than five present patterns (six or more) prompts a deliberate composition/copy review. Report the observed count and the combination creating the generic impression, not a pass/fail taste score. Five or fewer does not prove quality. The additional tells below guide judgment without silently changing the original threshold.
- Distinguish an intentional, brief-supported choice from an unexamined default. Preserve explicit user choices and existing brand constraints; explain relevant exceptions rather than automatically removing them. A requested headline sheen, grid, icon or three real programmes can be appropriate.
- Repair the underlying page purpose, hierarchy, proof or interaction. Do not merely exchange purple for beige, Inter for a serif, or bento cards for another fashionable template. State the three highest-impact changes, then make the authorized corrections. Do not add a new approval step.
- Fabricated claims and misleading or broken functionality are correctness problems regardless of count. Remove unsupported proof or label honest concepts/previews; never invent replacement clients, usage, testimonials or performance. A labelled concept image is not a live product screenshot.

## The 30 tells

1. Purple, or purple fading into blue, as the default palette.
2. A glowing orb behind the hero.
3. A dot-grid background.
4. Gradient headline text.
5. A sparkle icon on everything related to AI.
6. A glass card floating over a gradient.
7. Every icon sitting in a tinted rounded square.
8. An undifferentiated bento grid with no reason for the grouping.
9. A clearly fake product screenshot presented as working product evidence.
10. Inter or Geist as the only typographic idea.
11. Standard Tailwind colours used without brand-specific decisions.
12. Fake testimonials paired with fake avatars.
13. “Trusted by” logos without real relationships supporting that claim.
14. Impressive user counts or uptime figures without evidence.
15. A “Powered by AI” badge in the navigation.
16. A waitlist form with no working destination or honest unavailable state.
17. “It's not X, it's Y” copy.
18. Em dashes used everywhere.
19. Empty descriptors such as “Fast. Simple. Powerful.”
20. Generic feature-name word pairs, such as “seamless workflow”, that say little about the action or outcome.
21. Three feature cards with mechanically identical text lengths.
22. “Get started” beside “Learn more”, without meaningful action labels.
23. Three pricing tiers with the middle automatically labelled “Most popular”.
24. The stock $9 / $29 / $99 pricing ladder.
25. An FAQ filled with questions the audience has no demonstrated reason to ask.
26. Generic hover/scroll effects that add no meaning or feel poorly timed.
27. Hero copy that never says what the product actually does.
28. Blank screens instead of appropriate loading, empty and error states.
29. A blog link implying published articles when there is no blog to read.
30. An unchanged starter/default favicon.

Do not use this list to invent prices, remove genuine programmes, claim popularity, or fabricate FAQs. Neutral ecosystem logos are different from client endorsements; wording must make the relationship clear. Explicitly labelled planned content is different from pretending content exists.

## Additional tells to look for

These extend the review qualitatively; they are not automatic failures.

- **A squeezed desktop layout on phones:** awkward heading wraps, tiny controls, clipped artwork or horizontal overflow, even if the desktop screenshot looks good.
- **A visual collage:** mismatched icon strokes, illustration styles, radii, shadows and image crops with no intentional hierarchy.
- **Everything gets equal emphasis:** every block becomes a card, every title is oversized, and nothing helps the visitor choose what matters.
- **Interactions that only look interactive:** buttons go to the same place, filters do nothing, forms always report success, or a demo resets without explanation.
- **Animations fighting the task:** scroll hijacking, slow reveals, moving click targets, unreadable text or no reduced-motion alternative. Movement should explain, orient or express the selected concept.
- **A happy-path-only interface:** long names, real data, validation failures, expired sessions and keyboard focus break the layout or flow. Test a representative awkward case, not only ideal sample content.
- **Copy that could belong to any company:** no specific audience, real task, concrete example, useful constraint or honest reason to choose this offer.
- **Starter debris:** template page titles, placeholder links, broken social icons, lorem ipsum, mismatched metadata or old product names left behind.

## Coding brief and handoff

When delegating a website build, include this reference with the page's purpose, approved visual anchors and actual proof. Ask the implementer to preserve intentional exceptions and report concrete findings from the rendered page, including mobile and a meaningful interaction state. Do not spawn an agent solely to recite this checklist.

Keep the review compact: observed patterns and locations; why their combination is generic or intentional; the highest-impact corrections made; any uninspected state. Functional testing and visual judgment remain separate. A checklist count is never proof that the page is distinctive or production-ready.
